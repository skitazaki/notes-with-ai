Privacy risk is the possibility that data processing will create adverse effects for people, groups, or society. A breach is one source of harm, but authorized collection, analysis, or disclosure can also be intrusive, unfair, or damaging. Harm-based analysis complements rules by asking what processing actually does to people.

## Where Harms Arise

Collection can create surveillance, coercion, or loss of autonomy. Processing can produce mistaken identification, exclusion, manipulation, or decisions based on inaccurate or inappropriate inferences. Dissemination can expose secrets, damage reputation, enable discrimination, or make a person vulnerable to fraud and physical danger. Intrusion can disturb private space, attention, or decision-making.

These categories often compound. Extensive collection enables inference; inference supports consequential decisions; disclosure makes the result difficult to contain. Effects can fall on households or communities even when records describe individuals.

## Assessing Risk

Describe the people affected, plausible events, severity, likelihood, reversibility, and distribution of consequences. Consider power imbalance and whether people can understand, avoid, challenge, or correct the processing. Low-probability events may still demand strong controls when consequences are severe.

Avoid reducing the assessment to whether data is legally sensitive or securely stored. Ask what could be learned, how it could be used, and what happens when data or models are wrong.

## Reducing Harm

Remove unnecessary purposes and data first. Then apply architectural separation, access and use controls, de-identification, human review, contestability, retention limits, monitoring, and incident response. Document accepted residual risk and the accountable decision maker.

## Summary

Privacy harms reveal why privacy cannot be equated with confidentiality or compliance. Starting from effects on people helps teams select controls that address the real processing context.
