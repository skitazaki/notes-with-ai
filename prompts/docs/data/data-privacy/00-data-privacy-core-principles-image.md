# Image Generation Prompt — Core Privacy Principles

Create a publication-quality conceptual diagram for the **Core Privacy Principles** section of the Data Privacy documentation hub.

Purpose:

Help readers grasp seven privacy principles as one connected model rather than as an isolated checklist. The diagram should show that the principles guide decisions across the entire data lifecycle, from the decision to collect information through its deletion.

Audience:

- Technology leaders, architects, data platform engineers, and governance practitioners
- Senior engineers who understand data systems but are not necessarily privacy specialists

Core message:

Responsible data use begins with a clear purpose, limits information to what is needed, makes processing understandable, gives people meaningful participation, assigns accountable owners, limits retention, and embeds privacy into design and defaults.

Required concepts:

Represent all seven principles with these exact labels and brief supporting phrases:

- **Purpose Limitation** — Use data for clear, specific purposes
- **Data Minimization** — Collect and use only what is needed
- **Transparency** — Make processing understandable
- **Accountability** — Assign ownership and maintain evidence
- **Individual Participation** — Enable awareness, choice, and correction
- **Retention Limitation** — Keep data only as long as needed
- **Privacy by Design and by Default** — Build privacy into systems and default settings

Composition:

- Use a large, simple central element labeled **Responsible Data Use**.
- Surround it with seven principle panels arranged in a balanced circular or heptagonal pattern. Distribute the panels evenly around the center with consistent angular spacing, a shared radius, and a near-identical visual footprint so no side feels crowded or sparse. The layout must let a reader see all principles at once without following a prescribed sequence.
- Connect every principle panel to the central element with restrained lines or bands, showing that each is a decision lens for the same goal.
- Use subtle connector cues to show relationships without turning the diagram into a flowchart:
  - purpose limitation informs minimization and retention
  - transparency supports individual participation
  - accountability and privacy by design/default enable the other principles

Visual hierarchy:

- The central message and seven principle labels must be readable at documentation-column width.
- Supporting phrases should be short, secondary, and readable; do not add paragraphs.
- Use one simple secondary icon per principle only when it improves scanning: target for purpose, small filter for minimization, eye for transparency, ownership checkmark for accountability, person with speech bubble for participation, clock or archive for retention, and blueprint or settings for privacy by design/default.
- Give every principle equal visual weight, including consistent panel sizing and similar spacing from neighboring panels. Do not imply that one principle replaces the others.

Style:

- Clean editorial infographic for technical documentation
- Crisp vector-style shapes, simple geometry, and careful whitespace
- Light background and high-contrast, accessible typography
- Restrained professional palette: deep blue or teal for the center and coordinated muted accents for the seven principles
- Flat design or very subtle depth; no glossy 3D rendering
- Vendor-neutral and timeless, with no product branding
- 16:9 landscape aspect ratio

Do:

- Make the seven principles feel mutually reinforcing and complete as a conceptual framework
- Keep the image calm, balanced, and scannable at a glance
- Preserve symmetrical outer spacing so the ring of panels reads as evenly distributed around the center
- Use concise English labels exactly as provided

Do not:

- Use a dense table, checklist poster, legal document, compliance certification, or system architecture diagram
- Represent the principles as a mandatory linear sequence or a maturity model
- Overload the figure with legal, regulatory, or jurisdiction-specific language
- Use tiny text, stock photography, vendor logos, lock-only privacy metaphors, or decorative surveillance imagery
- Suggest that consent alone, security alone, or any individual control is equivalent to privacy
